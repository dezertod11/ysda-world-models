\begin{abstract}
Generative world-action models predict action chunks together with future
observations and values. This makes both value-based selection and
sample-consensus selection attractive at inference time, but agreement need
not imply that a useful action is present among the candidates. We investigate
this distinction using a frozen Cosmos Policy on a matched LIBERO-PRO
evaluation subset. Across 199 configurations and six deployed controllers,
neither a controller-scaled medoid nor reference consensus adaptations
establish a statistically convincing improvement over joint max-value
selection. A complementary frozen-state pilot executes eight candidates from
each of 36 snapshots: max-value succeeds in 18 snapshots and the empirical
candidate oracle in 19, while all eight tested branches fail in 17 snapshots.
These outcomes localize a limitation of selection on the sampled support,
subject to stochastic continuation and finite-sample uncertainty. Separate
repeated-continuation tests find variable labels for one third of saved
candidates and no pooled gain from an early fresh-observation intervention.
An outcome-based selector evaluated on held-out suffix seeds does not improve
over max-value at eight candidates. Separate
shared-prefix and perception-backed recovery studies show benefits in narrower
settings, without establishing general transfer. The findings motivate
separating candidate ranking, candidate coverage, and the value of new
observations when evaluating world-action planning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
World-action models offer a unified interface for predicting actions and
their possible consequences. Cosmos Policy embeds actions, future state, and
value into the latent generation process of a pretrained video model
\citep{cosmos_policy}. At deployment, however, a controller must still choose
which sampled chunk to execute and how long to wait before observing again.
Two plausible rules are to select the highest predicted value and to select
an action representative of the model's sampled distribution. These rules
answer different questions: predicted utility and distributional agreement.

Consensus selection has established precedents in language-model decoding
\citep{self_consistency} and generative robot policies \citep{keystone,kdpe}.
Its appeal does not imply that a confident mode is physically successful.
Likewise, an accurate offline prediction-error estimator need not improve
closed-loop control. A useful evaluation must distinguish a poor choice
among good candidates from a candidate set containing no observed successful
continuation, and must account for the effect of fresh observations.

We study these distinctions through three complementary views: a matched
deployed-controller comparison under Object, Environment, and Position
perturbations; a frozen-state candidate-outcome diagnostic; and contextual
evidence from separately controlled feedback and recovery studies. The first
two support conclusions about the tested selectors and candidate pools, not a
claim of a new state-of-the-art planner. The positive recovery results concern
different interventions and populations and are not pooled into the main
benchmark score. This separation is central to interpreting the evidence.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{World models and generative control.}
Diffusion Policy distinguishes prediction and execution horizons in receding
horizon control \citep{diffusion_policy}. Diffuser formulates planning as
trajectory generation \citep{diffuser}. TD-MPC2 and Dreamer investigate
world-model learning and control across diverse environments
\citep{tdmpc2,dreamerv3}. Our setting instead fixes the Cosmos Policy
checkpoint and studies inference-time decisions. Results from online model
learning or different action representations are not directly comparable.

\paragraph{Consensus and density-based selection.}
Self-consistency aggregates sampled reasoning outcomes in language models
\citep{self_consistency}. KeyStone selects a representative action from a
dominant cluster, with a guard against unnecessary clustering
\citep{keystone}. KDPE uses a manifold-aware kernel over action endpoints
\citep{kdpe}. We treat these as close prior art, not as novel components of
our method. Our reference implementations adapt their selection rules to
Cosmos and native LIBERO commands; they do not reproduce every sampling,
batching, or experimental choice of the original papers.

\paragraph{Uncertainty and failure-oriented evaluation.}
Velocity-field ensemble disagreement has been studied for flow-based VLA
uncertainty and active adaptation \citep{flow_uq}. UNISafe connects uncertainty
to latent safety filtering \citep{unisafe}, while StressDream explores
plausible high-impact outcomes of video world models \citep{stressdream}.
Stochastic samples from one checkpoint are not interchangeable with a trained
ensemble. Moreover, failure prediction, safe intervention, and improved task
success require distinct evaluations. We follow the broader principle of
reporting uncertainty and consistent protocols rather than relying on
aggregate point estimates alone \citep{rliable}.

\paragraph{Representation, memory, and conditioning.}
Don't Blind Your VLA studies visual alignment during policy fine-tuning
\citep{kachaev_blind_vla}, whereas muVLA isolates recurrent memory and
execution-cadence effects in partially observable manipulation
\citep{kachaev_mu_vla}. VLA Grounder optimizes input instructions while
freezing the action policy \citep{kachaev_vla_grounder}; this changes the
candidate distribution rather than only ranking an existing pool.
Act2Answer separates knowledge-grounded choices from motor execution
\citep{kachaev_act2answer}. These studies motivate distinguishing sources
of failure before choosing an intervention. Their training procedures and
benchmarks differ from our frozen Cosmos selector comparison; we do not
treat their scores as directly comparable baselines.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}
At query $q$, let $x_q$ contain the real camera observations, robot
proprioception, and instruction supplied to the policy. Let $s_q$ denote the
full simulator state, which is used only for offline replay and auditing, not
as an additional privileged policy input. The evaluated inference mode samples
\begin{equation}
 (A_q^{(i)},\widehat{o}_{q+T}^{(i)},\widehat{z}_{q+T}^{(i)},V_q^{(i)})
 \sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid x_q;\xi_{q,i}),\quad i=1,\ldots,K,
 \label{eq:candidates}
\end{equation}
where $A_q^{(i)}\in\R^{T\times7}$ is an action chunk, $\widehat{o}$ contains
predicted future images, $\widehat{z}$ is predicted future proprioception,
and $V$ is the decoded scalar value. Here $\xi_{q,i}$ controls model sampling;
it is conceptually separate from scene initialization and continuation RNG.
These quantities are generated jointly in the evaluated \emph{parallel}
mode. This is not the autoregressive action $\to$ future state $\to$ value
planning procedure evaluated in the Cosmos paper.

The controller selects an index $i^*$, executes the first $H\le T$ actions,
and obtains new real observations. A query is a model invocation at a control
boundary, not an individual simulator frame. The main comparison uses
$T=H=16$ and $K=4$, except for its $K=1$ control. The pilot uses $K=8$.
No inference about new observations is available to the selector during an
open-loop chunk.

\section{Consensus Planning}
\label{sec:method}
We evaluate a controller-scaled medoid alongside simpler and literature-based
selectors. The selected trajectory remains a sampled action chunk; we do not
execute an arithmetic average of potentially incompatible modes.

\subsection{Native Action Representation}
Each LIBERO action consists of translation command $u_t\in\R^3$, rotation
vector $\omega_t\in\R^3$, and gripper command $g_t\in\R$. For distance
computation, commands are clipped componentwise to $[-1,1]$. The induced
position and orientation proxies are
\begin{align}
 \bar p_t^{(i)} &= \sum_{\tau=1}^{t}c_p u_\tau^{(i)},\label{eq:position}\\
 \bar R_t^{(i)} &= \exp([c_r\omega_t^{(i)}]_\times)
                   \bar R_{t-1}^{(i)},
 \quad \bar R_0^{(i)}=I,\label{eq:rotation}\\
 b_t^{(i)} &= \begin{cases}1,&g_t^{(i)}\ge0,\\-1,&g_t^{(i)}<0.\end{cases}
 \label{eq:gripper}
\end{align}
Here $[\cdot]_\times$ is the skew-symmetric matrix associated with a
rotation vector. The scales $c_p=0.05$ and $c_r=0.5$ follow the controller
convention, with nominal
translation and rotation units of metres and radians. These integrated
command proxies are \emph{not} measured poses, forward-kinematic predictions,
or guaranteed trajectories of the robot under contact. Native 3D rotation
vectors must not be interpreted as a continuous 6D rotation representation.

\subsection{Control-Aware Distance Between Chunks}
For aligned execution times, define
\begin{align}
 \vartheta(R,S)
 &=\arccos\left(\clip\left(\frac{\tr(RS^\top)-1}{2},-1,1\right)\right),\\
 d_t(i,j)
 &=\frac{\|\bar p_t^{(i)}-\bar p_t^{(j)}\|_2}{c_p}
 +\lambda_r\frac{\vartheta(\bar R_t^{(i)},\bar R_t^{(j)})}{c_r}
 +\lambda_g\ind[b_t^{(i)}\ne b_t^{(j)}],\label{eq:action-distance}\\
 D_H(i,j)
 &=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{H}\gamma^{t-1}d_t(i,j)}
         {\sum_{t=1}^{H}\gamma^{t-1}}.\label{eq:chunk-distance}
\end{align}
The evaluated configuration sets $\lambda_r=0.5$, $\lambda_g=0.25$,
$\gamma=0.95$, $H=16$, and alignment window zero. Thus the score aggregates
disagreement across the executable prefix, with larger weights on earlier
steps. It is a geometric dissimilarity, not a calibrated failure probability
or an estimate of epistemic variance.

\subsection{Medoid Selection and Closed-Loop Execution}
For $K>1$, the medoid rule is
\begin{equation}
 i^*=\argmin_i\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\ne i}D_H(i,j).
 \label{eq:medoid}
\end{equation}
The implementation averages over all $K$ entries including the zero diagonal;
this positive rescaling leaves the ranking unchanged. Equal scores are broken
by larger predicted value and then smaller candidate index. Consequently,
the distance is value-independent, but the evaluated controller is not
strictly value-free in a tie. The $K=1$ control executes its sole candidate.
After $H$ actions, the controller queries the model using fresh observations.

The raw-medoid reference uses Euclidean distance between flattened native
chunks. KeyStone-style uses a guarded cluster/medoid rule; the KDPE adaptation
uses a kernel on induced OSC endpoints. The value baseline selects
$i_V=\argmax_i V_q^{(i)}$ from the jointly generated candidates. These are
deployed selection rules, not guarantees that the selected action succeeds.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
\paragraph{Matched deployed comparison.}
We use a valid subset of the LIBERO-PRO Object task suite
\citep{libero,libero_pro}, covering all ten tasks under three perturbation
factors: 50 Object, 50 Environment, and 99 Position cases per method.
One planned case, Position y0.5/task1/init1, is excluded uniformly because
its initialization asset is empty. The resulting 199 matched configurations
yield 1,194 rollouts across six methods; they are not 1,194 independent
initial scenes. All methods use five denoising steps and a 280-step
execution budget. This subset is not the full official benchmark.

\paragraph{Estimands and statistical reporting.}
For factor $f$ with $n_f$ cases and terminal success labels $Y_{m,f,e}$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{SR}_{m,f}=\frac{1}{n_f}\sum_{e=1}^{n_f}Y_{m,f,e},\qquad
 \mathrm{MacroSR}_m=\frac13\sum_f\mathrm{SR}_{m,f}.
 \label{eq:macro}
\end{equation}
Micro-SR pools all 199 labels and has different factor weights. We report
paired macro differences with task/init-cluster bootstrap intervals, and
rescue/harm counts relative to max-value. McNemar tests use discordant episode
outcomes, with Holm correction across the five baseline comparisons; they
are not a separate test of the equal-factor macro estimand.

\paragraph{Replay and candidate controls.}
Initial simulator states match to $10^{-9}$, but bitwise-identical query-zero
$K=4$ pools occur in only approximately 31--46\% of the comparisons against
max-value. The deployed study therefore does not isolate a selector applied
to exactly the same pool. We retain this limitation and separately analyze
saved candidate branches rather than attributing every difference solely to
the selection formula.

\paragraph{Frozen-state opportunity pilot.}
The pilot contains 36 snapshots from 18 case/task/init configurations, at
queries 0 and 3 ($t=0$ and $t=48$), with Object and Position perturbations.
These configurations share ten underlying task/init clusters. Each snapshot has
eight saved $H=16$ candidates, for 288 terminal branches. A common $K=1/H=16$
continuation policy follows each executed candidate within the remaining
budget. All 36 pools pass strict replay. Let $Y_i(s)$ be the measured terminal
outcome of a branch and define
\begin{equation}
 O_K(s)=\max_{i\le K}Y_i(s),\qquad
 G_K(s)=O_K(s)-Y_{\argmax_{i\le K}V_i}(s).
 \label{eq:oracle}
\end{equation}
This oracle is an offline diagnostic, never an online feature. The $K=4$
analysis uses the first four members of each saved $K=8$ pool; it is not a
full-episode $K=4$ controller evaluation. The present labels use one
continuation realization per branch, so oracle availability remains
conditional on these samples and suffix outcomes.

\paragraph{Repeated suffixes and matched feedback.}
A subsequent development experiment reuses all 36 snapshots and eight
saved candidates with three new suffix seeds, yielding 864 open-loop
branches. For each snapshot's original max-value candidate, two additional
arms execute its first eight actions and then sample once from either the
fresh observation or the archived observation, using the same extra-query
seed. Fresh8 executes the first eight new actions; stale8 executes the
last eight, aligned to the original interval. All arms return to the same
K1/H16 continuation and absolute-time seed schedule at $t+16$.
The total is 1,080 branches, excluding six smoke branches. Both declared
contrasts use task/init-cluster intervals and exact sign flips, with Holm
correction. Head/tail chunk differences limit a pure information-value
interpretation. This is a single snapshot intervention, not a controller
that re-queries every eight steps throughout an episode.

In Table~\ref{tab:pilot}, 16 mixed $K=8$ pools do not imply 16 opportunities
to improve max-value: it already selects a successful branch in 15 of those
pools. Only one snapshot has an observed successful candidate that max-value
misses. The empirical selection opportunity is therefore $1/36=2.78$ pp for
these saved outcomes. In 17 snapshots all tested candidates fail.
This is not a universal upper bound on planning and does not establish
irreversibility of those simulator states. Additional candidates, different
continuations, or feedback may change their outcomes. Repeated suffix seeds
are required before interpreting these observations as stable candidate
success probabilities.

\subsection{Repeated Continuations Change the Interpretation}
All 1,080 branches completed. Saved H16 endpoints replayed exactly in all
288 checks, and all 108 recorded videos passed decoding and frame-count
checks. Of 288 candidate chunks, 96 (33.33\%) have both success and failure
across the three new suffix seeds. Six of the pilot's 17 all-fail pools now
contain an observed success; 11 remain all-fail. These observations concern
stochastic policy continuation and residual numerical variability, not
calibrated epistemic uncertainty or state irreversibility.

The original K8 max-value candidate succeeds in 44/108 repeated branches
(40.74\%), compared with 41/108 for fresh8 (37.96\%) and 45/108 for stale8
(41.67\%). Fresh8 minus stale8 is $-3.70$ pp, with a 95\% cluster interval
of $[-20.51,9.72]$ pp and 13 rescues versus 17 harms. Fresh8 minus open16
is $-2.78$ pp, with interval $[-16.67,9.17]$ pp and 13 rescues versus 16
harms. Both Holm-adjusted values are 1.0. A pooled improvement is not
established; these intervals do not establish equivalence or universal harm.

Selecting the highest empirical candidate success rate and evaluating it on
the same three suffixes produces 55.56\% success, an optimistic $+14.81$ pp
relative to max-value. A post-hoc leave-one-suffix-out diagnostic instead
chooses on two repeats and evaluates on the third, breaking training ties
by frozen value and then index. Across all folds it achieves 44/108, exactly
matching max-value, with five rescues and five harms; the cluster interval
for the difference is $[-5.95,6.86]$ pp. This privileged diagnostic is not a
deployable learned selector, nor does its null result prove that ranking
is impossible with more reliable labels. It demonstrates why an apparent
within-sample oracle gain must not be reported as a planner improvement.

\subsection{Feedback and Recovery Provide Narrow Positive Evidence}
In a separate shared-prefix experiment on Object task 0 at query 4, fresh
observation feedback increases terminal success from 46/100 to 64/100
(50 init clusters), a difference of $+18$ pp with cluster interval
$[4,32]$ pp and 31 rescues versus 13 harms. This result concerns a specific
decision point, not a universal observation schedule.

A separate online RGB-based regrasp study on selected Position cells improves
a 40-case untouched-init holdout from 13/40 to 24/40, or $+27.5$ pp with
interval $[12.5,42.5]$ pp, 12 rescues, and one harm. Its subsequent transfer
estimate on seven new cells is $+14.3$ pp with interval $[-2.9,31.4]$ pp,
which fails its frozen gate. These findings motivate recovery and feedback
as mechanisms worth testing, while preventing a claim of established broad
transfer. Their populations and interventions differ from the main selector
benchmark, so their gains are not added to Table~\ref{tab:main}.

\section{Limitations and Next Tests}
The study uses one frozen backbone and a restricted benchmark subset.
The deployed comparison does not have fully identical candidate pools.
The pilot and follow-up are development diagnostics with correlated
snapshots; three new suffixes still give noisy candidate success estimates.
Geometry-based agreement is not calibrated confidence,
and simulated task success is not equivalent to real-world safety. Positive
feedback and recovery effects remain conditional on selected settings;
fixed times such as $t=72$ must not be generalized without new evidence.

The next controlled test should replicate the few persistent candidate
advantages with new suffixes and initial states, separate local contact
consequences from later failures, and test changed proposals or targeted
recovery where sampled support remains unsuccessful. The present results
do not justify a broad coefficient sweep or promoting always-early feedback.

\section{Conclusion}
On the evaluated Cosmos/LIBERO-PRO subset, consensus selection does not
establish a reliable gain over joint max-value selection. Frozen-state
branches further show why mixed candidate outcomes alone overstate the
available selection opportunity, while suffix replication exposes noisy
action labels and optimistic within-sample selection. Narrow feedback and recovery results point
to a different class of interventions, but their transfer remains unresolved.
A credible improvement to world-action planning must demonstrate not only a
plausible score, but useful candidate support, reproducible closed-loop gains,
and benefits that survive matched cost and held-out conditions.

\section{Decision-Time and Post-Execution Quantities}
For fixed-observation action samples $A^{(i)}\in\R^{T\times d}$, one possible
sample-disagreement statistic is
\begin{equation}
 U_A=\frac{1}{Hd}\sum_{t=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{d}
 \sqrt{\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{i=1}^{K}
     (A^{(i)}_{t,j}-\bar A_{t,j})^2},\qquad
 \bar A_{t,j}=\frac1K\sum_iA^{(i)}_{t,j}.
 \label{eq:uncertainty-example}
\end{equation}
This equation defines an illustrative sample standard-deviation aggregate,
not the medoid distance and not a claim that all historical metrics used
this denominator. Every reported estimator must separately specify $H$, the
degrees-of-freedom convention, action scaling, and averaging axes.
For a fixed query, $U_V^{\rm range}=\max_iV_i-\min_iV_i$ measures the range
across candidates, not temporal variation across the episode. Disagreement
among repeated entries within one latent output is another distinct axis and
must not be counted as independent model samples.

Actual future prediction errors such as
$\|\widehat z_{q+T}-z_{q+T}\|_2$ require execution and are unavailable for
selecting the chunk that produced them. They can be retrospective labels or
training targets for an online surrogate, but using the measured error as an
input at query $q$ would leak future information. Candidate-conditioned
uncertainty also differs from diversity between different candidate actions.

\section{Repeated-Continuation Diagnostics}
With $R$ continuation seeds, let $Y_{ir}(s)$ denote measured terminal success.
An empirical candidate success rate is
\begin{equation}
 \hat p_i(s)=\frac1R\sum_{r=1}^{R}Y_{ir}(s).
\end{equation}
The plug-in quantity $\max_i\hat p_i(s)$ is optimistic when the same noisy
replicates choose and evaluate the best candidate. A held-out repeat split
is required for an outcome-based oracle estimate. Snapshots and repeats from
the same task/init group must stay together during training and evaluation.

For paired intervention outcomes, two distinct targets are
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_{\rm obs}=\E[Y_{\rm fresh8}-Y_{\rm stale8}],\qquad
 \Delta_{\rm deploy}=\E[Y_{\rm fresh8}-Y_{\rm open16}].
\end{equation}
The former controls for an additional stochastic query, while the latter
measures the deployed intervention relative to commitment. Their cost and
information contracts must be reported rather than inferred from labels.
