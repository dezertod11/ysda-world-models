\begin{abstract}
Generative robot policies produce multiple plausible action chunks, but
agreement among predictions need not identify a successful action. We examine
test-time selection through complementary evaluations of action-space and
decoder-space medoids on MIMIC-Video, GR00T N1.7, Xiaomi Robotics 0, and Cosmos
Policy. We preserve the distinct benchmark populations and execution
contracts rather than pooling their success rates. Three-seed GR00T results
show mean decoder-medoid gains of 4.51 percentage points on SIMPLER and 3.65
on INT-ACT, but the corresponding Xiaomi effects are negative. In a matched
Cosmos transfer study, decoder medoid and joint-value selection each achieve
112/180 successes, with seven rescues and seven harms. Decoder selection
strongly favors particular fixed noise seeds, motivating controls that
separate representation agreement from seed identity. Complementary feedback
and physical-recovery studies distinguish selecting another prediction from
obtaining another observation or changing the physical state. Large historical
recovery gains are local: a corrected-runtime broad comparison finds no
significant aggregate improvement. These results motivate a controlled
evaluation of state risk, candidate quality, and intervention benefit as
different quantities, not a claim of a universally better planner.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Diffusion and flow-based robot policies generate temporally coherent actions
by transforming stochastic noise into action chunks \citep{diffusion_policy}.
World-action models additionally predict future observations, potentially
providing a way to evaluate an action before execution
\citep{cosmos_policy,mimic_video}. These interfaces invite test-time scaling:
sample several continuations and choose a reliable one without changing the
policy weights. However, similar samples can share the same mistake, an
accurate failure monitor may not rank available actions, and requesting a
new observation can change both computational cost and control behavior.

We study these distinctions through two complementary experimental programs.
The first evaluates action-space and decoder-space medoids across multiple
generative policies and distribution shifts. The second transfers decoder
selection to Cosmos and compares changes to candidate selection, feedback
frequency, and physical recovery. These programs share a scientific question
and some algorithmic components, but not an interchangeable task population.
Our comparisons therefore remain within matched model--benchmark contracts.

Self-consistency and representative-sample selection are established ideas
\citep{self_consistency,keystone,kdpe}. Our contribution is not the invention
of medoids. Instead, we examine three practical questions:
\emph{Does agreement improve closed-loop success across seeds and models?}
\emph{Does a selector exploit state-dependent quality or fixed noise identity?}
\emph{How does selection differ from feedback and physical correction?}
We report positive, null, and negative comparisons together with the controls
needed to interpret them. This separates useful scoped findings from claims
of broad robustness that the data do not support.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Generative policies and inference-time selection.}
Diffusion Policy models action trajectories directly
\citep{diffusion_policy}; MIMIC-Video and Cosmos connect video representations
to robot control \citep{mimic_video,cosmos_policy}. GR00T and Xiaomi Robotics 0
provide additional generative VLA interfaces \citep{groot17,xiaomi0}.
KeyStone uses shared-context sampling and a guarded cluster-medoid selector
\citep{keystone}; KDPE uses geometry-aware density estimation
\citep{kdpe}. Our global medoid, sample counts, action adapters, and seed
schedules differ from those systems. Accordingly, our results are not a
reproduction or refutation of their full published protocols. Small
selector overhead also does not imply negligible multi-sample inference cost.

\paragraph{Prediction reliability and recovery.}
Ruan et al. connect action-state consistency to success and study future
consensus, including deceptively consistent low-dynamics failures
\citep{future_compatible}. BadWAM demonstrates action--imagination
decoupling under adversarial perturbations \citep{badwam}.
We examine natural and benchmark-induced failures, rather than adversarial
attacks. Sentinel separates consistency from task progress
\citep{sentinel}; Rewind-IL combines failure detection with recovery
\citep{rewind_il}. These works motivate distinguishing the detection of
difficulty from the benefit of a particular intervention. Our physical
recovery consumes actions in the reached state; simulator branching is only
an experimental measurement tool, not an online ability of the robot.

\paragraph{Evaluation under distribution shift.}
SIMPLER evaluates manipulation policies in simulation, INT-ACT probes
generalization boundaries, and LIBERO and its extensions vary manipulation
conditions \citep{simpler,intact,libero,libero_pro,libero_plus}.
We keep standard LIBERO, LIBERO-PRO, LIBERO-Plus, SIMPLER, and INT-ACT
separate. Confidence intervals and repeated-seed comparisons must also
respect their experimental unit \citep{rliable}; an episode count is not
the number of independent tasks or trained models.

\section{Problem and Methods}
\subsection{Candidate generation and execution}
At query $q$, let $x_q$ denote the available real observations, robot state,
and instruction. A frozen policy generates candidates
\begin{equation}
A_q^{(i)}=G_\theta(x_q,\xi_{q,i}),\quad
A_q^{(i)}\in\R^{T\times d},\quad i=1,\ldots,K.
\end{equation}
The controller selects one candidate and executes its first $H\le T$
actions. The metric may use a distinct scoring prefix $L\le T$.
Distinguishing $T$, $L$, and $H$ prevents a change in execution horizon
from being misattributed to a better selector.

For Cosmos, inputs are external and wrist RGB, nine-dimensional proprioception,
and a precomputed instruction embedding. Each joint generation predicts a
$16\times7$ action chunk, future observations/proprioception, and value.
Our value baseline selects $i_V=\argmax_i V_i$ from these joint samples.
It is not the original paper's rollout-finetuned autoregressive
$a\rightarrow s'\rightarrow v$ planning procedure \citep{cosmos_policy}.
After execution, ordinary queries receive actual images, not imagined ones.

\subsection{Action-space medoid}
Write an action as translation $u$, orientation $R$, and gripper command $g$.
After the model-specific action adapter, the control-aware distance is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
d(a,b)={}&\frac{\|u_a-u_b\|_2}{\sqrt3}
+\frac{0.5}{\pi}\theta(R_aR_b^\top)\\
&+0.25\,\ind[\operatorname{sign}(g_a)\ne\operatorname{sign}(g_b)],
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\theta(R)=\arccos(\operatorname{clip}((\operatorname{tr}R-1)/2,-1,1))$.
For a model with 6D rotation output, $R$ is obtained by orthonormalization;
Cosmos uses native three-dimensional rotation-vector commands instead.
Translation is measured in the adapter's command units: the $\sqrt3$
factor alone is not a physical normalization across embodiments.
The temporally weighted distance and selected index are
\begin{align}
D_L(i,j)&=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{L}0.9^{t-1}d(a_{i,t},a_{j,t})}
{\sum_{t=1}^{L}0.9^{t-1}},\\
i_A&=\argmin_i\frac1{K-1}\sum_{j\ne i}D_L(i,j).
\end{align}
The chosen chunk is an actual sample, not the average of incompatible motions.
This is a global medoid, distinct from KeyStone's guarded dominant-cluster
medoid. In the Cosmos transfer control, $L=5$ while $H=16$; this historical
setting is explicitly retained rather than described as an executable-prefix
match that was not used.

\subsection{Decoder-space medoid}
The decoder variant compares aligned hidden action representations at the
last captured conditional denoising call. For temporal tokens $z_{i,t}$,
\begin{align}
D_Z(i,j)&=\frac{\sum_t w_t[1-\cos(z_{i,t},z_{j,t})]}{\sum_t w_t},\\
w_t&=4\ \text{for }t\le4,\quad w_t=1\ \text{otherwise},\\
i_Z&=\argmin_i\frac1{K-1}\sum_{j\ne i}D_Z(i,j).
\end{align}
Cosine norms are lower-bounded by $10^{-12}$; exact ties select the first
index. GR00T excludes its state token and scores the generated action-token
sequence, although only four actions are executed on SIMPLER. MIMIC extracts
its action segment with its own decoder adapter. These implementations
must not be interpreted as identical hidden tensors across architectures.

Cosmos requires a spatial adaptation. Its action latent frame has
$16\times28\times28$ scalar elements containing 112 copies of a
$16\times7$ action block. The last DiT hidden grid provides 196 spatial
tokens of width 2048. A patch is not one action time. Let $C_{pt}$ count
decoded scalars belonging to time $t$ within patch $p$. We use
\begin{align}
\widetilde w_p&=\frac{\sum_t C_{pt}w_t}{\sum_{r,t}C_{rt}w_t},\\
D_Z(i,j)&=\sum_p\widetilde w_p[1-\cos(z_{i,p},z_{j,p})].
\end{align}
Features are captured read-only, without additional denoiser calls.
The prefix-eight ablation sets weights of times nine through sixteen to zero.

\subsection{Sampling contracts and uncertainty}
The decoder experiments use $K=3$ and predefined seed groups. The inspected
GR00T wrapper and Cosmos transfer reuse each candidate's seed on every query,
so observations change while noise identity is retained. This is a valid
fixed-noise policy family, not fresh independent noise at every query.
Our recovery experiments instead use
$s_{q,i}=s_{\mathrm{rollout}}+1000q+i$ with $K=4$.
Comparisons across these contracts are not pooled.

Medoid centrality is neither variance nor a calibrated failure probability.
For stochastic action vectors, a descriptive componentwise spread can be
written as $U_A=(Hd)^{-1}\sum_{t,k}\operatorname{Std}_i A_{i,t,k}$;
the first-action version fixes $t=1$. A scalar state-level spread does not
rank candidates: subtracting the same $\lambda U(x_q)$ from every $V_i$
leaves the value argmax unchanged. Candidate ranking requires a
candidate-dependent score. Moreover, disagreement between distinct joint
action--future samples mixes different plans; it is not repeated uncertainty
about one fixed action's consequence.

\subsection{Feedback and physical recovery}
Feedback replaces an unexecuted suffix using a fresh observation. Our
scheduled physical recovery additionally localizes the target in external RGB
using a separately trained DeepLabV3--ResNet50 heatmap localizer. The maximum
heatmap location is projected through calibrated camera geometry and an
object-conditioned height estimate. The gate checks finite values, heatmap
score range, workspace/reach, and target--end-effector separation between
0.08 and 0.50 m. Score range is a heuristic, not calibrated confidence.
The localizer uses 388 calibration images from 194 groups; consequently,
the full system is not training-free even though Cosmos remains frozen.

An accepted correction opens and retreats, obtains another localization,
approaches and descends, closes, and lifts. The stages allow at most
$3+7+6+4+5=25$ physical actions, all charged to the 280-action episode budget.
The policy then continues with $H=8$. We compare this to H8 continuation,
H16 continuation, and sensing/requery controls. Historical assessment times
56, 72, and 88 are scheduled ablations, not a universal learned failure time.

\section{Experimental Design}
\subsection{Separate populations, matched comparisons}
SIMPLER-Bridge contains four tasks with 24 episodes each. The official
INT-ACT Object-OOD subset contains eight tasks with 24 episodes each; the
16-task development extension is excluded. For GR00T and Xiaomi, the
three reported primary seeds are 1, 2, and 3. Candidate groups are
$(1,999,998)$, $(2,997,996)$, and $(3,995,994)$. MIMIC uses the additional
groups stated in Table~\ref{tab:other}; they are not interchangeable replicates.

The additional LIBERO experiments include standard Spatial with 100 episodes,
GR00T's PRO Spatial aggregate with 400 episodes across four OOD slices,
and a first-100 Background Textures slice of LIBERO-Plus. These are not
our Cosmos PRO Object-suite benchmark. The latter uses Object, Environment,
and Position perturbations with 50, 50, and 99 valid cases, respectively.
The decoder transfer grid instead contains 180 matched cases, 60 per factor,
and three seed groups; its Position support is restricted to x0.2/y0.2.
No headline SR is computed by merging these populations.

All Cosmos decoder comparisons generate 16 actions with five denoising
evaluations per candidate and execute H16 unless labeled H8. A common initial
state and cached first-query pool are shared between compared methods.
Later observations and candidate pools naturally diverge after different
actions. The 1440-rollout decoder program includes 720 main H16 rollouts,
360 fixed-candidate controls, and 360 H8 controls, not 1440 independent tasks.

\subsection{Outcomes, uncertainty, and audit}
The primary outcome is the simulator's terminal task-success condition;
timeouts are failures, whereas technical errors are missing data, not
negative outcomes. We report counts and within-population SR. Micro-SR
weights episodes equally; factor-macro SR weights perturbation factors equally.
For paired outcomes,
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathrm{SR}_{\mathrm{micro}}=
\frac{N_{\mathrm{rescue}}-N_{\mathrm{harm}}}{N}.
\end{equation}
Cross-model seed means are descriptive. Wilson intervals for individual
binomial proportions do not replace a paired interval for a method's effect.
Exact McNemar tests are reported where paired outcomes were available;
repeated observations of shared task/init groups require clustered analysis.
Cosmos decoder intervals resample tasks within factors while retaining seed
repeats. Broad recovery uses stratified task/init clusters, 5000 bootstrap
replicates, 20000 sign flips, and Holm correction for four planned contrasts.
An interval containing zero does not establish equivalence.

An audit identified missing solver warmstart and sensor-cache state in an
earlier simulator snapshot implementation. Snapshot v2 stores complete MuJoCo
integration state and observable caches. The broad v2 experiment includes
995 completed rollouts and explicit no-intervention replay checks. Historical
scoped and shared-prefix studies are labeled separately; a broad v2 audit
does not retroactively validate those measurements.

\subsection{Decoder agreement does not improve Cosmos ranking}
In the matched H16 Cosmos grid, first-candidate selection achieves 105/180,
action medoid 110/180, and both decoder medoid and max-value 112/180.
Decoder versus value yields seven rescues and seven harms, with task-cluster
95\% interval $[-3.33,3.33]$ points. Fixed second and third candidates achieve
110/180 and 109/180. The results do not establish extra utility from
hidden-space selection over these seed controls.

The hidden selector nevertheless makes structured choices. For the three
seed groups, a different favored seed is selected in 84.46\%, 83.25\%, and
81.18\% of queries. Equal weighting of episodes preserves the pattern
(85.90\%, 84.99\%, 82.70\%), so it is not explained solely by longer failed
episodes contributing more queries. This supports a hypothesis that hidden
centrality partly reflects fixed noise identity. It does not prove that noise
fully causes the observed performance or that every hidden selector behaves
this way. Pooling candidate indices across groups would hide the preference.

\subsection{More feedback is not an unconditional improvement}
On the matched 120-case H8 subset, max-value H16 achieves macro-SR 65.00\%
versus 59.44\% for H8. The difference interval includes zero. Full decoder
H8 achieves 57.78\%; prefix-eight decoder reaches 59.44\%, matching its
H8 value control rather than surpassing it. H8 roughly doubles logical
candidate evaluations, with factor-wise ratios of 1.93--2.07. These are
logical counts, not clean wall-clock latency measurements on the shared server.

A separate historical shared-prefix study on one Object task records
46/100 to 64/100 successes after a fresh-observation suffix replacement,
with 31 rescues, 13 harms, and init-cluster interval $[4,32]$ points.
It uses 50 initial states and two repeats per state. This local finding
and the broader H8 comparison concern different supports and are not
contradictory. The historical replay contract requires a separate v2 audit.

\subsection{Physical correction: strong scope, limited transfer}
On eight development-selected, calibration-familiar Position cells,
historical recovery comparisons record 13/40 to 24/40, 6/40 to 25/40,
and 16/64 to 41/64 successes relative to H8 continuation. The last effect
is $+39.06$ points, with 27 rescues, two harms, and recorded init-cluster
interval $[29.69,48.44]$. A matched retreat/requery control achieves 8/40
versus full recovery's 25/40. This supports a physical-correction mechanism
within that population, subject to the scoped runtime check; it is not a
latency-matched decomposition of every maneuver stage.

The transfer boundary is substantial: calibration-unseen x0.3 cells achieve
0/64 for continuation and 1/64 for recovery. In the corrected broad benchmark
(Table~\ref{tab:broad}), scheduled recovery gains only 0.68 macro points
over H16, with interval $[-3.00,4.36]$, nine rescues, and six harms.
Its mean query count is 23.00 versus 13.86 for H16. The event-triggered arm
performs no interventions in 199 cases; its equal SR is a coverage failure
of the tested trigger, not evidence for effective adaptive recovery.

\section{Discussion: What Should a Reliability Score Predict?}
For a fixed state $s$, candidate pool, horizon, and continuation policy,
define $Q(s,A_i)$ as the success probability after executing candidate $i$
and following that continuation. The selector's shortfall decomposes as
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
1-Q(s,A_{\hat i})={}&[1-\max_i Q(s,A_i)]\\
&+[\max_i Q(s,A_i)-Q(s,A_{\hat i})].
\end{split}
\end{equation}
This identity separates limitations of the given pool/continuation from
loss due to selecting its wrong member. It is not a new theorem or an
already estimated oracle bound in every study. Reliable estimation requires
repeated suffix outcomes and held-out evaluation of a chosen candidate;
selecting and evaluating an oracle on the same outcomes is optimistic.

Three distinct targets follow: state risk $P(Y=0\mid s,\pi)$, candidate
quality $Q(s,A_i)$, and recovery advantage
\begin{equation}
\Delta_R(s)=E[Y\mid\operatorname{do}(R),s]
-E[Y\mid\operatorname{do}(C),s].
\end{equation}
A high failure-detection AUROC does not establish within-pool ranking
or positive recovery advantage. Our geometric gate does not estimate
$\Delta_R$. Similarly, prediction error against an actual future observation
is only available after execution and cannot be used as a pre-execution
score for that same chunk. Predicted T16 futures must not be compared to
H8 observations without temporal alignment.

The present evidence favors targeted controlled tests over additional
coefficient searches on already inspected failures. Hidden-seed preference
should be tested with fixed-candidate and fresh-noise controls; candidate
quality should be measured within a common state/pool; recovery should be
evaluated against its observation and action-budget controls. The positive
findings in different cohorts do not yet prove that physical recovery
rescues precisely the states in which consensus is uninformative.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}
The study combines complementary but heterogeneous model--benchmark
experiments. Several cross-model results are supported by aggregate records
rather than a common reprocessed episode-level archive. Three seed groups
provide limited evidence about sampler generalization; some positive effects
were identified during development. The P3 scope is calibration-dependent,
its historical timing is scheduled, and its strongest historical measurements
require corrected-runtime confirmation. We do not claim SOTA, universal
consensus failure, a new backbone, or deployment on physical hardware.

Within these limits, the central finding is useful for designing inference-time
methods: agreement, candidate quality, feedback value, and physical recovery
are not interchangeable. Cross-model seed controls and matched interventions
make their distinction observable. Reported local gains motivate further
work, but neither a favorable seed nor an accurate monitor alone establishes
a better closed-loop controller.
